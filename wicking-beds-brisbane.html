Wicking beds in Brisbane: a beginner's guide

If your veggies wilt every summer afternoon even though you watered that morning, a wicking bed could change your gardening. A wicking bed is a raised garden bed with a water reservoir in the bottom. Water rises up into the soil by capillary action, so roots drink from below while the surface stays drier.

Wicking beds have become popular across South-East Queensland for good reason. They cut down hand-watering through the hot months. But Brisbane also gets heavy wet-season downpours, so a badly built bed can turn into a swamp. This guide covers how they work, when they're worth it, sizes for small spaces, and how to set one up so it suits our climate.

How a wicking bed works

A wicking bed has four layers, from the bottom up:

  1. A waterproof base or liner. The bed itself, or a pond liner inside a frame.
  2. A reservoir. Usually scoria, gravel, or plastic wicking cells, sitting 15–30 cm deep and holding water.
  3. A separation layer. Geotextile fabric that keeps soil out of the reservoir.
  4. The soil. About 30 cm of good-quality mix that pulls water up from below.

You fill the reservoir through an inlet pipe. An overflow outlet at the top of the reservoir lets extra water out. The overflow is the most important part for Brisbane. Without it, a summer storm fills the whole bed and drowns the roots.

Is a wicking bed worth it in Brisbane?

Good reasons to get one:

  • You're away a lot, or you forget to water in summer.
  • You're growing thirsty crops: tomatoes, cucumbers, zucchini, eggplant, leafy greens and herbs.
  • You're on a sunny courtyard or rooftop where normal pots dry out by lunchtime.
  • You'd like to use less water. Suppliers claim large savings over top watering, though actual results depend on your sun, mulch and crop.

Reasons to skip one:

  • Your space is shady and stays damp anyway.
  • You mainly grow plants that hate wet feet, like rosemary, lavender and succulents. Wicking beds keep the root zone moist all the time.
  • You're on a balcony with a weight limit. A filled bed with water is very heavy (see below).

Sizes for Brisbane courtyards and balconies

  • Balcony or narrow courtyard: a slim trough or a bed about 1.2 m × 0.45 m. Some Australian makers sell wicking beds in exactly this size for small spaces.
  • Townhouse courtyard: about 1.1–1.2 m square. Plan on roughly 300 litres of soil and a reservoir of about 100 litres.
  • DIY: any raised bed at least 50–60 cm deep can be converted with a liner, scoria or wicking cells, geotextile and pipes.

Weight warning: a large wicking bed full of wet soil and water can weigh several hundred kilograms. Some manufacturers list around 400 kg for a 1 m² unit. On a balcony, check your building's load limits with your body corporate or an engineer first. On a balcony, start small with a wicking trough or self-watering planters instead.

Setting one up for Brisbane conditions

1. Put the overflow in the right place. It should sit at the top of the reservoir layer, below the soil. In a downpour, extra water drains out instead of saturating the root zone.

2. Keep the soil layer to about 30 cm. Capillary action only lifts water so far. Much deeper and the top of the bed stays dry. Much shallower and it stays soggy.

3. Use an open, free-draining mix. Heavy clay soil wicks poorly and goes sour. A premium potting mix blended with compost works well. Our potting mix guide covers what to look for.

4. Mulch heavily. A 5 cm layer of sugar cane mulch stops surface evaporation, which is the main loss in a Brisbane summer.

5. Water from the top at first. Newly planted seedlings have shallow roots that can't reach the wicked moisture yet. Water them from above for the first 2 weeks.

6. Ease off in the wet season. From January to March, you may not need to fill the reservoir at all. Check with your finger or a moisture meter before topping up.

7. Flush it occasionally. Fertiliser salts can build up in a closed system. Once or twice a year, overfill from the top until water runs out of the overflow for a few minutes.

What to grow in a wicking bed in SEQ

  • Spring–summer: cherry tomatoes, cucumbers, zucchini, eggplant, capsicum, chillies, Thai basil, snake beans, Brazilian spinach. For timing, see What to plant in Brisbane in October.
  • Autumn–winter: lettuce, kale, silverbeet, broccoli, coriander, parsley, peas and strawberries.

Fruit fly is still a problem in a wicking bed. Use exclusion bags on tomatoes and chillies. Our tomato guide and chilli guide explain how.

FAQ

Do wicking beds get mosquitoes? Not if the reservoir is sealed and the inlet pipe is capped. Put fine mesh over the overflow outlet as well, because Brisbane mosquitoes will find any open water.

Can I build a wicking bed on a balcony? Only a small one, and only after checking load limits. A wicking trough or self-watering planter is a safer choice for most apartments.

How often do I fill the reservoir in Brisbane? In a hot, dry spell, every 3–7 days depending on the size, sun and crop. In the wet season, often not at all.

Can I use garden soil? It's better not to. Heavy soil wicks poorly and compacts. Use a quality potting or raised-bed mix.

Do wicking beds cause root rot? They can if there's no overflow, or if the soil layer is too shallow. With a correctly placed overflow and about 30 cm of open mix, the top of the root zone stays aerated.
