Growing tomatoes in pots in Brisbane: timing, pots and fruit fly

Tomatoes are the plant almost everyone wants to grow, and the one that frustrates Brisbane gardeners most often. Plants look great, flower, and then the fruit splits, rots or gets stung by fruit fly. Most of these problems come down to timing and a few simple habits.

Timing matters more than anything

In cooler parts of Australia, tomatoes are a summer crop. In South-East Queensland, mid-summer is actually the hardest time to grow them. Very hot days and warm, humid nights can stop flowers setting fruit, fungal leaf diseases spread quickly, and Queensland fruit fly is at its most active.

The more reliable windows for SEQ are:

  • Late summer to autumn (roughly February to April), for picking through the mild winter and early spring. For many Brisbane gardeners this is the best season of all.
  • Late winter to early spring (roughly July to September), for picking before the worst of the summer heat.

You can plant in October, but choose cherry tomatoes, which handle heat and humidity much better than big slicing varieties, and expect the crop to slow as summer arrives. If you're in October now, the October planting guide lists other crops that suit the season.

Choosing a variety for pots

  • Cherry and small-fruited tomatoes are the best all-rounders for SEQ pots. They tend to set fruit more reliably in warm weather and ripen quickly, which gives fruit fly less time to find them. Cherry tomato seeds are inexpensive and easy to raise.
  • Dwarf or determinate types stay compact and suit smaller pots and windy balconies.
  • Tall (indeterminate) types keep growing and fruiting, but need a big pot and a tall stake or cage.

Pot size

Tomatoes are thirsty, hungry plants, and a pot that's too small is the most common cause of trouble. Small pots dry out fast, which leads to stressed plants, split fruit and blossom end rot.

  • Dwarf types: at least 20 litres.
  • Cherry and tall types: 30–40 litres or more. A large fabric grow bag works well and is lighter to move. Look for 40-litre fabric grow bags.

Use a good-quality potting mix rather than garden soil, and leave a few centimetres at the top for mulch. The guide to potting mix for Brisbane heat explains what to look for on the bag.

Sun, wind and support

Tomatoes need at least six hours of direct sun to fruit well. In spring and autumn, give them the sunniest spot you have. In the hottest months, a little afternoon shade can help.

Put in support when you plant, not later, so you don't damage roots. A tomato cage suits bushy cherry types, while tall types are easier on a sturdy stake. Tie stems loosely with soft plant ties so they don't cut in as the plant grows. On high, windy balconies, a lower-growing variety in a heavy pot is often the better choice.

Watering: consistency is everything

Uneven watering causes most fruit problems in potted tomatoes:

  • Split fruit often follows a dry spell and then a heavy watering or a storm.
  • Blossom end rot (a dark, sunken patch on the bottom of the fruit) is linked to the plant not moving calcium into the fruit properly, which is usually caused by irregular watering rather than a lack of calcium in the mix.

Check pots daily in warm weather using the finger test, and water deeply when the top few centimetres are dry. Mulch helps a lot. If you're often away, tomatoes are one of the crops where self-watering pots really earn their keep. Avoid wetting the leaves, especially in the evening, because it encourages disease. The watering mistakes guide has more on this.

Feeding

Tomatoes need steady feeding in pots. A common mistake is giving them too much nitrogen, which produces a big leafy plant with few flowers.

  • Mix a controlled-release fertiliser into the potting mix at planting, following the label.
  • Once flowers appear, switch to a fertiliser made for tomatoes or fruiting vegetables, which is higher in potassium. A tomato fertiliser used at label rates every week or two is plenty.

Pollination on a balcony

Tomato flowers mostly pollinate themselves, but they need a little movement to release pollen. Outdoors, wind and bees usually do this. On a sheltered balcony, gently tapping or shaking the flower trusses every day or two while they're open can improve fruit set.

Queensland fruit fly

Queensland fruit fly lays eggs in ripening fruit, and the larvae turn it to mush from the inside. Small puncture marks on the skin are often the only sign until you cut the fruit open. Ways to manage it in pots:

  • Exclusion bags or netting. Covering fruit trusses with fruit fly exclusion bags, or the whole plant with fine netting, is the most reliable non-chemical method for a small number of plants.
  • Pick early. Harvest fruit as soon as it starts to colour and let it finish ripening indoors. It still tastes good, and it spends less time on the plant.
  • Clean up. Collect any fallen or stung fruit, seal it in a bag and put it in the bin. Don't compost it.
  • Traps and baits. These can help monitor numbers and reduce them, especially if neighbours use them too. Follow the product instructions.

Leaf diseases

Humidity encourages fungal leaf spots and blights, which usually start on the lower leaves as yellow patches with brown spots. To reduce problems:

  • Remove the lowest leaves once the plant is established, so none touch the mix.
  • Water the mix, not the foliage, and preferably in the morning.
  • Give plants space and airflow. Don't crowd pots together.
  • Pick off affected leaves early and put them in the bin.
  • Use fresh potting mix each season rather than replanting tomatoes in the same mix.

Quick checklist

  • Plant in late summer to autumn or late winter to early spring for the best results.
  • Choose cherry or compact varieties for pots.
  • Use a 30–40 litre pot or grow bag with fresh potting mix.
  • Stake at planting.
  • Water consistently and mulch.
  • Feed for fruit once flowering starts.
  • Bag fruit or pick early to beat fruit fly.
