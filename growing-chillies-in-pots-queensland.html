Growing chillies in pots in Queensland: a balcony guide

If you only grow one fruiting plant on a Queensland balcony, make it a chilli. Chillies love heat, cope with humidity, and in frost-free South-East Queensland a healthy plant can keep producing for several years. Here's how to get the most from one in a pot.

When to plant

Chillies grow best in warm weather, so spring is the ideal time to plant in SEQ, from about September through to early summer. Plants put in now establish quickly and crop heavily from summer into autumn. Because we rarely get frost near the coast, you can plant through much of the year, but growth slows in winter, so spring plantings give the best results.

Seeds need warmth to germinate, which is why they can be slow in winter and fast in spring. Sow them in a fine seed raising mix and keep them moist and warm. Seedlings from a nursery give you a head start of several weeks.

Choosing a chilli

There are hundreds of varieties, and almost all of them grow well in SEQ. A rough guide for pots:

  • Small, hot chillies such as bird's eye types are compact, very productive and among the toughest.
  • Medium chillies like cayenne and jalapeño are versatile in the kitchen and well suited to pots.
  • Habanero and other very hot types are slower to grow and ripen but love our heat.
  • Mild and large chillies and capsicums are thicker-fleshed and more attractive to fruit fly, so they need more protection.

A chilli seed variety pack is an easy way to try a few and see which ones you like.

Pot size and potting mix

Chillies don't need huge pots, but bigger pots mean steadier moisture and bigger harvests.

  • Compact varieties: 15–20 litres works.
  • Most chillies: 25–30 litres is a comfortable size for a plant you want to keep for a few years. A 25-litre fabric grow bag is light and drains well.

Use fresh, premium potting mix. Chillies hate sitting in soggy mix, so good drainage matters, especially in the wet season. See choosing potting mix for Brisbane heat and pots that work on a Brisbane balcony.

Sun and position

Give chillies as much sun as you can, ideally six hours or more. They're one of the few edibles that can handle a hot north- or west-facing balcony. In a severe heatwave, flowers may drop and leaves can scorch, but plants usually recover when temperatures ease. A little afternoon shade on the very hottest days doesn't hurt.

Taller plants laden with fruit can topple in wind, so use a bamboo stake in larger pots.

Watering

Water when the top few centimetres of mix are dry, then water deeply. Chillies are fairly forgiving, but repeated drying out stresses them and reduces flowering. In winter, they need much less water. Overwatering in cool weather is a common way to lose a plant. The watering mistakes guide covers the basics.

Feeding

Feed lightly while plants are young, then switch to a fertiliser for fruiting plants once they start flowering. Too much nitrogen gives lots of leaves and fewer chillies. A liquid tomato and vegetable fertiliser every couple of weeks during the warm months, at label rates, is usually plenty, or use a controlled-release fertiliser every few months.

Pests

Queensland fruit fly

Fruit fly lays eggs in chillies and capsicums, especially thicker, milder varieties. Signs include small puncture marks, soft spots and fruit that rots from the inside. Cover fruiting branches with fruit fly exclusion bags or fine netting, pick fruit promptly, and bin (don't compost) any stung fruit. Small, very hot chillies are often less affected, but not immune.

Aphids, whitefly and mites

These sap-sucking pests gather on new growth and under leaves, especially in warm, dry weather. Hose them off, squash small clusters, and if numbers build up, use a horticultural oil spray or insecticidal soap labelled for vegetables. Spray in the cool of the morning or evening and follow the label, because oils can burn leaves in hot sun.

Possums and birds

Birds sometimes take ripe red chillies, and possums may nibble young plants. Fine mesh covers help. See stopping possums eating balcony plants.

Common problems at a glance

  • Flowers drop without setting fruit: usually extreme heat, irregular watering or too much nitrogen. Fruit set normally picks up when the weather eases.
  • Yellowing lower leaves: often hunger in an older pot, or overwatering in cool weather. Check the mix before feeding.
  • Pale, stretched plants: not enough sun. Move the pot to the brightest spot you have.
  • Leaves curling and sticky: look underneath for aphids or whitefly.
  • Soft, rotting fruit with tiny holes: fruit fly. Bag fruit and bin the damaged ones.

Harvesting

You can pick chillies green or wait for them to ripen to their final colour, which is usually hotter and sweeter. Regular picking encourages more flowers. Use scissors or snips rather than pulling, which can break branches. Wash your hands (or wear gloves) after handling hot varieties, and keep your hands away from your eyes.

Keeping a chilli going for years

In SEQ's frost-free climate, chillies are perennials. Older plants often produce earlier in the season and more heavily than new seedlings. To keep a plant going:

  • After the main crop finishes, or in late winter, prune it back by about a third to a half to encourage fresh growth.
  • Refresh the top layer of potting mix, or repot into fresh mix every couple of years.
  • Resume feeding when new growth starts in spring.

Eventually plants get woody and less productive. Save seed from your favourite open-pollinated variety, or take a cutting, and start again.
